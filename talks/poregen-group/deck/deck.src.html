Motivation

Why generate porosity?

Pores control the strength of a composite part. Real scans are slow and few. We want many volumes with known pores.

train pore-segmentation networks

test NDT methods on known pores

build digital twins of coupons

ask "what if" for porosity and shape

XCTX-ray computed tomography: a 3-D image of how much X-ray each point absorbs. Dark = low density = pore or air.
NDTnon-destructive testing: inspecting a part (e.g. by ultrasound) without cutting it.
digital twina virtual copy of a real part, used to predict how it behaves.

~50 s. Porosity is the main manufacturing defect in these laminates and it lowers the mechanical properties. Every real volume costs a scan and a segmentation. A generator that gives many volumes, with the porosity we ask for and with labels, is useful for data, for testing methods and for "what if" questions.

Diffusion · the idea

Destroy with noise, then learn to undo it

Adding noise needs no learning. Removing it is what a neural network learns. To make a new image, start from pure noise and remove it step by step.

→
forward: add Gaussian noise
t = 0
Gaussian noiserandom values from a bell curve, added independently to every pixel.
step thow much noise has been added: t = 0 is the clean image, t = 1000 is pure noise.
diffusion modela network trained to undo the noise one step at a time.

~40 s. Intuition first. Going right, we add a little Gaussian noise many times until nothing of the slice is left. This direction needs no learning. Going back, a network predicts what noise to remove at each step. If it can do that at every noise level, it can start from pure noise and end at a new, realistic image.

Animation: the closed-form forward process on a real slice (same scan as the title, another region), cosine schedule, computed in the browser.

PoreGen
Diffusion · forward process

Adding noise: one formula for any step

We never add noise 1000 times. One formula gives the noisy image at any step t directly: keep a fraction of the image, fill the rest with noise.

xt = √ᾱt · x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε   clean image, scaled down, plus noise, scaled up
x₀the clean image.
εa pure-noise image of the same size.
xtthe image after t steps of noise.
ᾱthow much of the clean image is left: 1 at t = 0, 0 at t = 1000. Its shape over t is the noise schedule.
SNRsignal-to-noise ratio = ᾱt ÷ (1 − ᾱt): signal power over noise power.

~80 s. Define every symbol before using it. x₀ is the clean image, ε a noise image, xt the noisy image at step t. ᾱt ("alpha bar") is the share of the clean image that is left; it falls from 1 to 0. The square roots keep the total brightness spread the same at every step.

Because this formula exists, training never simulates the chain: pick a random t, draw a noise image, build xt in one line. Top row: the same slice at six steps. Bottom left: ᾱt for our schedule (a cosine curve). Bottom right: the signal-to-noise ratio on a log scale. At t = 1000 it is exactly 0: nothing of the image is left. The marker follows the highlighted panel.

Schedule: our model's configuration (cosine, T 1000, s 0.008). ᾱT = 3.75·10⁻³³ was measured on this schedule (value recorded in the model configuration file). Panels are computed in the browser on a real slice. Mathematically this is the Gaussian q(xt | x₀) = N(√ᾱt x₀, (1 − ᾱt) I) of Ho et al. 2020.

Diffusion · reverse process

Removing noise: a network guesses the noise

Training: show the network a noisy image and the step t; it must output the noise that was added. Generating: subtract a little of the guessed noise, again and again.

loss = average of ( ε − εθ(xt, t) )²

Ho, Jain & Abbeel — Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, NeurIPS 2020

t = 1000
εθthe neural network; θ are its weights. Input: xt and t. Output: its guess of the noise ε.
lossthe number training makes small: here the squared difference between the true and the guessed noise.
samplinggenerating: start from pure noise x1000 and apply the network step by step down to x₀.

~60 s. The network is called εθ because it outputs a guess of ε, and θ are the weights we train. Training is ordinary supervised learning: we made the noisy image ourselves, so we know the right answer, the noise. The loss is the squared error between the true and the guessed noise.

To generate, start from pure noise. At each step, the network guesses the noise; we remove a small part of it and get a slightly cleaner image. After all steps we have a new image. The animation on the right only illustrates the idea on a real slice; it uses the true noise, not a network. Our model actually predicts a different quantity, v — slides 10 and 11 explain what and why.

Reference verified: Ho, Jain, Abbeel, NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2006.11239.

Diffusion · why it works

Why it works: images on a probability landscape

Picture every possible image as a point. Real-looking images are rare: they sit on hills of high probability. Noise pushes an image downhill; the network learns the way back up.

noise level σ = 0.10
  • White rings: two hills — two kinds of realistic image (a toy example in 2-D).
  • Arrows: at every point, the direction that goes uphill. This arrow is called the score.
  • The noise added to an image points downhill. So the network's noise guess, turned around, is the score.
  • Amber dots: start anywhere, follow the arrows, end on a hill = a realistic sample.
probability density p(x)how likely an image x is. High on realistic images, near zero on random ones.
score ∇ log p(x)the gradient of log p: the uphill direction at x, and how steep it is.
noise level σmore noise smooths the landscape: arrows point roughly to the centre. Less noise: arrows point to each hill.

~80 s. This is the key intuition for the ML side. Every image is a point in a very high-dimensional space. Real images occupy tiny regions with high probability. Adding noise moves a point away from those regions, so the noise vector points "downhill". A network trained to predict that noise therefore knows, at every point, which way is uphill — this is the score.

The animation: the arrows are the score of a toy 2-D landscape with two hills; σ is the amount of noise. With much noise the landscape is smooth and the arrows point to the centre; as σ falls, they point to each hill. The particles follow the arrows and end on the hills. That is exactly what sampling does in image space.

Toy Gaussian mixture computed in the browser. The formal results are on the next slide.

Diffusion · the theory in three lines

The theory in three results

Each result in words first, then as an equation.

1 · A network that removes noise has learned the uphill direction (Vincent 2011).

score = ∇ log pt(xt) ≈ − εθ(xt, t) / √(1 − ᾱt)

2 · The best guess of the clean image is the noisy image moved one step uphill (Tweedie's formula, Efron 2011).

best guess of x0 = ( xt + (1 − ᾱt) · score ) / √ᾱt

3 · With infinitely small steps, adding noise is a continuous random motion. Running it backwards in time needs only the score (Song et al. 2021).

dx = [ f(x, t) − g(t)² · score ] dt + g(t) dw̄
gradient ∇the vector of slopes: the direction in which a function grows fastest.
stochastic differential equation (SDE)an equation for a motion with a random part: drift f and random kicks g·dw.
DDPM, DDIMtwo recipes for taking the backward steps; next slide.

~70 s. Read the words, not the symbols. (1) Denoising and estimating the score are the same task: the noise guess, flipped and scaled, is the score. (2) Given a noisy image, the best estimate of the clean image is the noisy image plus a step along the score — this is how each step makes a clean-image guess. (3) Song et al. showed that the whole noising process is a stochastic differential equation, and its time reversal needs only the score. DDPM and DDIM are two ways to discretise that reverse equation.

References verified: Vincent, Neural Computation 23(7), 2011; Efron, JASA 106(496), 2011; Song et al., ICLR 2021, arXiv:2011.13456. In result 3, f is the drift, g the noise strength and w̄ a backward-time Brownian motion.

Diffusion · sampling

Sampling: 1000 noisy steps or 50 clean ones

The same trained network can be used in two ways. DDPM takes 1000 small steps and adds fresh noise each time. DDIM adds none and takes big steps: 50 are enough.

DDPM — 1000 steps, random pathDDIM — 50 steps, smooth path, same network
  • Both end on the hills: realistic samples.
  • DDIM is deterministic: the same starting noise always gives the same image.
  • 20× fewer network calls. We generate with DDIM, 50 steps.
DDPMdenoising diffusion probabilistic model (Ho et al. 2020): the original sampler, with fresh noise at each step.
DDIMdenoising diffusion implicit model (Song, Meng, Ermon 2021): no fresh noise, steps can be skipped.
deterministicno randomness during the steps; only the starting noise is random.

~50 s. Same toy landscape. Grey: DDPM paths, jittery because fresh noise is added at every one of 1000 steps. Amber: DDIM paths, smooth, 50 steps, using the same network. Both reach the hills. We sample with DDIM at 50 steps because it is 20 times cheaper and, for us, as good.

References verified: Ho et al. NeurIPS 2020; Song, Meng, Ermon, ICLR 2021, arXiv:2010.02502. Toy paths computed in the browser. Operating point (DDIM-50): generation settings of the final model's evaluation.

Diffusion · conditioning and guidance

Conditioning: telling the model what to make

We give the network extra inputs, the condition — for us, the porosity we want. Guidance then pushes the result further towards that condition.

In training, the condition is hidden at random: the network learns both "a coupon" and "a coupon with 2 % porosity".

At sampling we ask both questions and step beyond the conditional answer:

guided = unconditional + s × ( conditional − unconditional )

Ho & Salimans — Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance, 2021

conditionan extra input the output must follow: requested porosity, ply angles, specimen shape.
unconditionalthe network’s answer when the condition is hidden (a learned "null" input).
guidance scale ss = 1: the plain conditional answer. s > 1: follow the condition more strongly.

~50 s. A model that only makes "some coupon" is not useful; we want to say what to make. The condition is fed as an extra input. Classifier-free guidance: during training the condition is sometimes replaced by a null token, so the same network can answer with and without it. At sampling we compute both answers and extrapolate along their difference with scale s. Later (slide 27) we show what s does to our porosity: asked for 0.02, s = 1 gives 0.0176 and s = 2 gives 0.0199.

Reference verified: Ho & Salimans, NeurIPS 2021 workshop, arXiv:2207.12598. Numbers: full evaluation of the final model, guidance cells (see slide 27 sources).

Diffusion · what the network outputs

What should the network output? Three choices

From a noisy image, a network can be trained to return the noise ε, the clean image x₀, or a mix of the two called v. With xt, any one of them gives back the other two.

xt = a · x0 + b · ε     v = a · ε − b · x0     with a = √ᾱt, b = √(1 − ᾱt), a² + b² = 1
t = 0
ε-predictionoutput the noise. The original DDPM choice.
x₀-predictionoutput the clean image directly.
v-predictionoutput v: mostly noise at small t, mostly (minus) the image at large t (Salimans & Ho 2022).

~70 s. The panels show, for one real slice and the step t shown below, the noisy input xt and the three possible targets. Watch them as t grows: ε is always pure noise; x₀ is always the clean image; v changes — at small t it looks like the noise, at large t it looks like the negative of the image.

They carry the same information: xt = a·x₀ + b·ε and v = a·ε − b·x₀, so knowing xt and one of them gives the others. What differs is how easy each is to learn and how errors are amplified. That decides the choice — next slide.

Images computed in the browser from a real slice with our schedule. Reference verified: Salimans & Ho, ICLR 2022, arXiv:2202.00512 (introduces v).

Diffusion · why we predict v

Why we predict v

At the last step the input is pure noise. Guessing the noise there is trivial and says nothing about the image — and turning that guess into an image divides by zero. v asks for the image itself.

From an ε guess: image = ( xt − b · ε guess ) ÷ a. At the last step a = 0 (exactly 6·10⁻¹⁷): any error is multiplied by 10¹⁶.

From a v guess: image = a · xt − b · v guess. No division. At the last step, image = −v guess.

Our schedule reaches zero signal at the last step. Training then ends at pure noise, exactly where sampling starts. Many older schedules left a faint trace of the image there (Lin et al. 2024).

a, bthe image and noise weights at step t: a = √ᾱt, b = √(1 − ᾱt). The arrow on the left turns from image to noise.
zero terminal SNRat the final step the signal-to-noise ratio is exactly 0: nothing of the image is left.
signal leaka schedule that ends with a little image left; training and sampling then start from different inputs.

~80 s. Left: a and b behave like cos and sin of an angle, so xt rotates from the image (right) to the noise (up) as t grows; v is the direction of that rotation. Right: the problem with ε. To get an image from a noise guess we divide by a. Near the end a → 0, so errors explode; at the very last step it is a division by zero. With v there is no division, and at the last step the network must simply output the image (with a minus sign).

Zero terminal SNR: our cosine schedule removes the image completely at the last step. That is good — sampling starts from pure noise, so training should end there too. Lin et al. showed many schedules end with a small "signal leak"; ours has none. But zero signal is exactly where ε fails. That is why our model predicts v. Bottom right: the SNR over the last 10 % of steps, ours (amber, falls to 0) against the classic linear schedule (grey, stays above 0).

Values: ᾱT = 3.75·10⁻³³ and √ᾱT = 6.12·10⁻¹⁷, measured on our schedule and recorded in the model configuration. Linear schedule: β from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02 over 1000 steps (Ho et al. 2020), computed in the browser. References verified: Salimans & Ho, ICLR 2022, arXiv:2202.00512; Lin et al., WACV 2024, arXiv:2305.08891.

Diffusion · latent space

Diffuse in a compressed latent, not in voxels

Diffusion on full 3-D volumes is too expensive. So we first train a network that compresses each 64³ patch 48 times, and run diffusion on the compressed version.

Rombach et al. — High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models, CVPR 2022

autoencodertwo networks trained together: an encoder E that compresses, a decoder D that rebuilds the input from the compressed code.
latentthe compressed code: here 4 numbers for each 4 × 4 × 4 block of voxels.
U-Netthe denoiser network type: it shrinks the input step by step, then grows it back, with shortcuts between equal sizes.
parametersthe trainable weights of a network (83 million here).

~50 s. This is the idea of Rombach et al.: train an autoencoder once, then run diffusion on its latent. One 64³ patch has 3 × 64³ = 786 432 input values; its latent has 4 × 16³ = 16 384, a factor 48. That is the same factor as Stable Diffusion's autoencoder (512² × 3 → 64² × 4). Every denoising step works on the small latent; the decoder runs once at the end.

Our model: 3-D U-Net with 82.9 M parameters (generation log of the final model); latent 4 × 16³ (autoencoder configuration). Stable Diffusion factor: CompVis model card, "downsampling factor 8, H×W×3 → H/8×W/8×4". Reference verified: Rombach et al., CVPR 2022, arXiv:2112.10752.

State of the art · porous media

Generating microstructures: what is missing

Earlier generators make realistic texture. None gives us all four things we need at once.

3-D GAN · Mosser et al. 2017

A GAN trained on 3-D CT of rock. No control input.

SliceGAN · Kench & Cooper 2021

A GAN that learns 3-D from 2-D images only. No control input.

Diffusion for microstructures · 2023–2024

2-D images (Düreth et al.), 2-D→3-D (Lee & Yun), 3-D porous media (Lyu & Ren).

Porosity-conditioned 3-D LDM · Naiff et al. 2026

One porosity value as the condition. A 2026 preprint adds a porosity map.

What we need, all at once

  • Large volumes — whole coupons, 1024 × 1024 × 192 and more
  • Local control — porosity where we ask, ply angles, shape
  • Already segmented — grey and material/pore/air labels together
  • Specimen geometry — surfaces, holes, notches
GANgenerative adversarial network: a generator makes samples, a critic tries to tell them from real ones; both improve.
LDMlatent diffusion model: diffusion on compressed data, as on slide 12.
conditioned / unconditionalwith / without an input that controls the output.

~60 s. GANs gave the first 3-D microstructures. SliceGAN removed the need for 3-D training data. Diffusion models have replaced GANs in quality, first in 2-D, then 3-D. The closest work to ours is Naiff et al. 2026: a latent diffusion model for 3-D porous media conditioned on porosity. Their 2026 preprint adds a porosity field for large volumes.

Our target is the combination on the right. I do not claim that no other work has any one of them; the point is to have all four in one model, for composites, with labels.

Model · autoencoder

Our autoencoder: grey in, grey and labels out

The encoder compresses grey image + pore map + air map into the latent. The decoder rebuilds the grey image and, for every voxel, a label.

mask / mapa 0/1 image: 1 where there is a pore (or air), 0 elsewhere.
↓4, ↑4each side shrinks 4× (64 → 16) in the encoder and grows back in the decoder.
μ, σ → zthe encoder gives a mean μ and a spread σ for each latent number; z is drawn from them (a variational autoencoder).
3-class segmentationfor each voxel, the probability of material, pore and air.

~50 s. Inputs: three 64³ maps — grey, pore mask, air mask. The encoder shrinks each side by 4 and outputs, for each of the 4 × 16³ latent numbers, a mean and a spread; the latent z is drawn from those (that is what makes it a variational autoencoder). The decoder grows z back to 64³ and has two heads: the grey image and three class probabilities per voxel.

Architecture: autoencoder configuration (3 input channels, z = 4, base 32 channels, two down/up blocks, 3-class head); 0.76 M parameters (generation log). Model in use: step-25 000 checkpoint.

Training the autoencoder: five terms

Each term checks one thing. Their weighted sum is the loss that training makes small.

~70 s. One line per term. L1 keeps the grey values. LPIPS — "learned perceptual image patch similarity" — compares slices through the eyes of an image-recognition network, so texture matters. Cross-entropy and Dice train the labels; Dice is 2 × overlap ÷ (size A + size B). KL keeps the latent close to a standard normal distribution, which suits diffusion; its weight is tiny. From step 15 000 a 3-D discriminator, a critic network, is added: the decoder is rewarded when the critic cannot tell its output from real patches. This is the Stable Diffusion autoencoder recipe, made 3-D. Bottom: when each term is on during training.

Recipe (decisions log and autoencoder configuration): L1 on the [−1, 1] grey scale, LPIPS weight 1.0 on 2-D slices, class cross-entropy and soft Dice weight 1.0 each, KL weight 10⁻⁶ summed, 3-D N-layer discriminator (3 layers, 64 filters, hinge loss), adaptive weight factor 0.5, discriminator start at step 15 000, Adam (0.5, 0.9), learning rate 5.76·10⁻⁴ at batch 128. References verified: Zhang et al. (LPIPS) CVPR 2018; Isola et al. (patch discriminator) CVPR 2017; Rombach et al. CVPR 2022.

Model · denoiser

What the denoiser sees

Besides the noisy latent, the network gets maps (what is where) and numbers (how much). Everything we can control enters here. It outputs v for the patch.

channelsmaps stacked as extra input layers, as red, green and blue are stacked in a photo.
injected by normalisationnumbers that scale and shift the network’s internal features at every layer.
ply orientationfibre angle θ at each depth, given as cos 2θ and sin 2θ so 0° and 180° match.
material envelopewhich latent cells are inside the specimen; the rest is air.

~70 s. Spatial inputs are concatenated as channels: the ply-orientation profile (cos 2θ, sin 2θ per depth), the material envelope (which cells are specimen and which are air), the six face neighbours' latents, a learned flag per neighbour (exists / unknown / outside), and each neighbour's own noise level. Scalars enter by adaptive group normalisation together with the timestep: the porosity request, the depth, and the distance from each of the six patch faces to the specimen surface.

The porosity request is per patch, so a map of requests gives local control. Every input is always present; there are no switches.

Channel count 4 + 2 + 1 + 24 + 48 + 48 = 127 (architecture document and generation log, in_ch = 127); 82.9 M parameters; 3-D U-Net base 128 channels, multipliers 1/2/4 (model configuration).

Neighbours, and dropping one face at a time

A large volume is made patch by patch. Each patch sees its six touching neighbours so the two sides continue each other. Training must show every case the generator meets.

exists — a neighbour latent is given (noised to its own level) unknown — a neighbour exists but is not made yet outside — the specimen ends on this side
  • per face, p = 0.10: one face becomes unknown
  • all six, p = 0.05: the null for guidance
  • without per-face dropout: a pore-depleted band at chunk boundaries
neighbourthe latent of the patch touching one face; it tells the network what is on the other side.
dropouthiding an input at random during training, so the network also learns to work without it.

~60 s. The cube is one patch. Each face has one of three states. In training, each face independently becomes "unknown" with probability 0.10, and all six together with probability 0.05 (that is the "no neighbour" condition used by guidance).

First, measure real against real

Two real crops of the same panel are not identical either. That distance is the floor. Every result is a ratio to it.

~50 s. A raw distance between generated and real means nothing alone: how far is "far"? So for every statistic we also compare two disjoint real crops from the same panel, at the same porosity. Ratio = distance(generated, real) / distance(real, real). Ratio 1: the generated set is as close to real as real is to itself — as close as the measurement can tell. Results · how we compare texture

Four ways to compare microstructure

Each turns a volume into a curve or a distribution. To compare real with generated we take the distance between them: W₁ — the area between the two cumulative curves — or, for images, FID.

~80 s. (1) Two-point correlation S₂(r): the probability that two points a distance r apart are both pore. It captures pore fraction, size and spacing at once. The plot shows real against generated at porosity 0.03. (2) Pore-size distribution: sizes of connected pores. (3) Ripley's K: for pore centres, how many other centres lie within r, compared with a random (Poisson) pattern; it measures clustering. (4) FID: the Fréchet distance between Inception-network features of 2-D slices, real against generated; it measures how the image looks.

W₁ — the Wasserstein-1 or "earth mover's" distance — is the area between the two cumulative distributions: how much mass must be moved, and how far, to turn one into the other.

Curves: full evaluation of the final model, microstructure, level 0.03, S₂ for generated and matched real crops. References verified: Torquato, Random Heterogeneous Materials, Springer 2002 (S₂); Ripley, J. Appl. Probab. 13(2), 1976 (K function); Heusel et al., NeurIPS 2017 (FID).

Baselines · what they are

Three baselines, three questions

Each one removes one idea of ours, so the comparison says what that idea buys.

SliceGAN

A 3-D generator; 2-D critics judge its slices along x, y and z. Unconditional. Question: does diffusion beat a GAN?

Pixel-space 3-D diffusion

The same diffusion recipe run directly on voxels, no autoencoder. Unconditional. Question: what does the latent buy?

Porosity-only latent diffusion

Our autoencoder and denoiser, but one porosity value as the only condition. Question: what do maps and neighbours buy?

~60 s. SliceGAN (Kench & Cooper 2021): a 3-D generator trained against 2-D discriminators on slices of the three axes; it needs only 2-D data. Pixel-space diffusion: our diffusion recipe (v-prediction, zero terminal SNR) on voxels directly; it tests the latent. Porosity-only LDM: the published recipe of Naiff et al. — porosity as the single condition — on our autoencoder; it tests our extra conditioning. The first two are unconditional, so they enter only the request-free comparisons.

Baselines as run on our data: SliceGAN 60 000 steps; pixel-space diffusion stopped by a 24 h cap at step 55 546, DDIM-200; porosity-only LDM on the final autoencoder's latents. Reference verified: Kench & Cooper, Nat. Mach. Intell. 2021; Naiff et al., Computers & Geosciences 206 (2026).

Limitations and next steps

Limitations

  • Decoder blur. Rebuilt patches keep about 3/4 of the real sharpness (fine-detail energy); FID stays 3× the floor.
  • Chunk seam. The standard sampler leaves 0.6–0.9 of the pores on chunk planes; the overlap removes it at extra cost.
  • Local field. Generated porosity patches stay similar only over about 50–60 voxels in-plane; in real coupons, over hundreds (correlation length).

Next

  • finish the overlapped-scheme evaluation; make it the default
  • pore-by-pore morphology against real coupons
  • baselines re-run on the final setup
  • a sharper decoder, without moving the latent space
